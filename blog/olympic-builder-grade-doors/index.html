The Olympic-era legacy: why so many 2008–2012 townhouse doors are failing right now.

Around the 2010 Olympics, builders across the Lower Mainland put up thousands of townhouses and low-rises, and nearly every one got a builder-spec garage door with the cheapest springs on offer. That was sixteen years ago. The math says those springs should be failing right about now. I am writing this from the passenger seat of the Tacoma between calls. They are failing on schedule.

Dale McRae
Lead Technician · Vancouver / Burnaby / North Shore / Tri-Cities / Surrey & east

This is the post I've been threatening to write since 2023, because I am tired of telling each customer the same story individually. Here is the story once, in writing, so I can text them this link instead of having the same conversation in their driveway for the fifth time this month.

Setting the scene: the Olympic-era building boom

Between roughly 2007 and 2012, Vancouver and the surrounding municipalities issued building permits for an unusual volume of multi-family housing. The Olympic Village got the headlines, but the garage doors went in out in the suburbs — Burquitlam, Brentwood, central Surrey, central Richmond, Willoughby, Morgan Heights. (Downtown towers mostly got one shared parkade gate, which is a commercial door and a different story.) Nobody keeps a tidy count, but the number of garage-equipped townhouses built in that 2008 to 2012 window across Greater Vancouver runs well into the tens of thousands.

Every one of those buildings put in garage doors. Every one of those doors had springs. And every one of those springs was rated to 10,000 cycles. Because that's the industry's base spec, and builder grade means base spec.

The cycle math

A garage door cycle is one open-and-close. A 10,000-cycle spring is rated to last 10,000 of those before fatigue failure. ("Rated" is generous — in our experience, real-world life runs anywhere from about 7,500 to 11,000 cycles depending on environment, lubrication, and luck.)

If the spring was installed in 2010 and the household runs 4 cycles a day, the math says the spring should have failed around 2017. If it's still hanging in 2026, you are statistically on borrowed time. That's not a sales pitch. That's literally what the rating means.

The two brands you'll see

Two brands show up again and again on doors from that window: Wayne Dalton and Garaga. Both made fine doors for the price point, the builder packages we see came with base 10,000-cycle springs, and both are on the same end-of-life curve.

Wayne Dalton in particular installed a lot of their TorqueMaster sealed-tube spring system during this period. The TorqueMaster Plus version uses plastic internal gears that don't age well. We have a whole separate post on the TorqueMaster situation — short version, the plastic gears strip and even a fresh repair tends not to last. The honest fix is conversion to standard torsion. We've done a lot of those in Burquitlam alone.

How to tell if you're an Olympic-vintage door

1. When was the building built?

Check the title or your strata documents. "Built 2009" or "2010" or "2011" — you're in the window. Even "2008" or "2012" — you're close enough.

2. Have the springs ever been replaced?

Ask the previous owner. Check your strata maintenance records. If the answer is "I don't know" or "I don't think so," assume not. The strata won't necessarily have tracked individual garage door spring changes per unit.

3. Look at the spring itself.

The big horizontal coil above the door. If it's silver-grey, that's galvanized. If it's black with a slight oily sheen, that's oil-tempered. If your 2010 builder put in galvanized in a damp Vancouver garage, the metallurgy is working against you. Oil-tempered lasts longer, period.

4. Look at the paint on the cones.

Winding cones are usually colour-coded for wind direction — red for right-wound, black for left-wound — and many springs also carry a paint stripe for wire size. If the paint is clean and crisp, the spring is probably younger than the building. If it's faded or flaking, the spring is probably original.

Where the failures are clustering right now

  • Burquitlam townhouses — a big build-out around the Como Lake and North Road / Lougheed corridor. Wayne Dalton TorqueMaster is heavily represented. I'm getting several calls a week here.
  • Brentwood / Lougheed townhouse rows — busy households, short driveways, the garage used as the front door. Plenty of these should have had high-cycle springs from day one. Most didn't get them. Cycle counts are brutal.
  • South Surrey / Morgan Heights — a lot of 2008-2012 townhouse stock on standard torsion. Failures are real but usually simpler — straight spring swaps.
  • Willoughby Heights — Langley's townhouse boom of the same era. Same story, different traffic.
  • Central Richmond and Central Surrey — townhouse complexes from the same years, often with fewer cars per unit. Lower cycle counts, so failures are arriving a bit later, but they're arriving.

What's the legacy actually worth

The 2010 Olympics gave us the Canada Line, an athletes' village that became condos, a brand reputation that's still cashed for tourism marketing, and a generation of housing nobody can afford. (Yes, I lived in an East Van basement suite for $1,500 in 2014 — you can stop telling me how cheap that was.) The garage door legacy is less photogenic, but it's quietly real: a whole generation of doors hitting end-of-life on roughly the same timeline. The good news is the second-generation install is almost always better than the first, because we're not bidding for a building — we're servicing a home.

What we put in instead

When we replace an Olympic-vintage spring set, we change four things in the spec:

  1. Oil-tempered, not galvanized. Lasts longer in the climate. Same install labour.
  2. Cables come with the two-spring tier free. Builder-grade cables are 16 years old too. They go now.
  3. Sealed bearings on the shaft. The original shaft bearings on most builder doors are unsealed and have grit in them by now. Sealed costs $40 in parts. Cheap insurance.
  4. High-cycle option offered honestly. If you're going to live in the place for another 10 years and you run 5+ cycles a day, 25,000-cycle springs make math sense. If you're going to sell in 2 years, the 10,000-cycle two-spring tier is the right answer.

The honest bit

I'm not going to pretend the Olympics ruined Vancouver garage doors. The Olympics did what they were going to do — they put a lot of housing up, fast, at minimum spec, and now we live with the consequences. It's the same story as the cheap windows, the cheap baseboards, and the dishwasher cut-out that nothing new quite fits. Builder grade is builder grade. The fix is to replace the part that fails with a better part.

If your building is 2008-2012 and your door is acting weird — the opener straining, the lift feeling heavier, a noise that wasn't there last year — book the call. The door doesn't lie. The math doesn't either.
